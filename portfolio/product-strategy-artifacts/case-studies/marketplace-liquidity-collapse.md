# When Removing a Supply Subsidy Collapsed GMV

*Simulated case study — fictional marketplace (Lantern), representative of real dynamics in two-sided marketplace economics.*

---

The unit economics finally looked right. Supply-side subsidy costs had been the drag on profitability for three years. Leadership approved removing them after the platform hit its GMV targets. Supply dropped 38% in 60 days. Recovery took eleven months.

---

## Context

Lantern was a services marketplace connecting independent contractors with business customers. The supply side — contractors — had been subsidized for four years through enhanced payment terms: contractors received 60% faster payment than the platform's standard payout timeline, funded by Lantern at a financing cost. The subsidy had been introduced at launch to attract supply when the platform had no demand density. By year four, the platform had 11,000 active contractors and was hitting GMV targets.

The CFO's position: the payment term subsidy was costing $2.2M annually and was no longer justified by the supply acquisition rationale. The platform had supply. The subsidy should be sunset.

The product team's position: remove it on a 90-day notification timeline, communicate clearly, and monitor supply-side churn.

The decision was made. The subsidy was removed.

---

## What Happened

Supply contraction began in week three of the notification period — earlier than the transition date. Contractors began reducing their availability on the platform before the change took effect. By day 60 post-transition, active contractor listings were down 38%. By day 90, overall GMV had declined 29%.

The GMV impact lagged the supply drop because demand-side customers initially worked through existing contractor relationships. Once those relationships were completed, customers began searching for availability and finding reduced inventory. Satisfaction scores on the demand side declined. Several enterprise customers put renewal decisions on hold.

---

## What the Analysis Got Wrong

**The subsidy was analyzed as an acquisition cost, not a retention mechanism.** The original rationale for the payment terms subsidy was supply acquisition — attract contractors when the platform had low demand density. By year four, acquisition was no longer the problem. The finance team correctly identified that the acquisition rationale no longer applied.

What the analysis missed: over four years, the faster payment terms had become the reason many contractors chose Lantern over competitor platforms. It wasn't in the acquisition data because it had stopped being an acquisition driver — it had become a retention driver. The contractors who remained on Lantern weren't there because of launch incentives; they were there because the faster payment terms fit their cash flow needs. Removing them created a switching trigger where none had existed.

**The supply-side concentration was underweighted.** The top 20% of contractors (by transaction volume) accounted for 63% of GMV. When the subsidy was removed, high-volume contractors had the most to lose and the most leverage to negotiate with competitors. They were also the ones competitors would most aggressively recruit. The analysis treated supply reduction as uniform; the actual reduction was concentrated in the highest-value segment.

**The 90-day notification window assumed rational decision timing.** The expectation was that contractors would continue operating normally until the transition date, then make a decision. What actually happened: the notification created a planning trigger. Contractors who were already marginally committed to the platform used the 90-day window to evaluate alternatives. The notification didn't give contractors time to adjust — it gave them a reason to start looking.

---

## What Recovery Required

The recovery strategy had two phases:

**Phase 1 — Stop the bleeding.** Within 45 days of the GMV decline becoming visible, Lantern reintroduced payment term flexibility as a voluntary program: contractors could opt into faster payment in exchange for a fee (essentially factoring their receivables). This wasn't the original subsidy — contractors paid for the faster terms rather than receiving them free — but it eliminated the hard stop that had triggered the supply exit. The uptake rate was 71% among active contractors.

**Phase 2 — Rebuild supply.** The platform had lost contractors to competitors who had recruited aggressively during the transition window. Winning them back required supply-side campaigns, enhanced onboarding for new contractors, and in several cases, direct negotiation with high-volume contractors who had partially migrated to competitors.

Full supply recovery to pre-transition levels took eleven months. The $2.2M annual savings from removing the subsidy was partially offset by recovery costs and by eleven months of compressed GMV growth.

---

## The Durable Lesson

In a two-sided marketplace, incentives that began as acquisition mechanisms often become retention mechanisms over time. The switch happens gradually and doesn't show up cleanly in acquisition funnel data — it shows up in the supply-side behavior you only observe when you remove the incentive.

Before removing a structural incentive, the right question is not "why did we add this?" It's "what would happen if we removed it, and which suppliers would leave first?" Running that analysis requires supply-side behavioral data (usage patterns, alternatives considered, price sensitivity) that most marketplace teams don't have because they stop collecting it once supply acquisition is no longer the primary problem.

The finance team's analysis was correct on what it measured. It measured the wrong thing.
