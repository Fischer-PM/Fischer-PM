# Eigenquestions: High-Leverage Strategy Questions

Rumelt's insight is this: strategy has three parts. First, diagnosis — the clear-eyed view of the real problem you're facing. Second, guiding policy — the specific stance that flows from that diagnosis. Third, coherent action — the initiatives that reinforce each other and the policy.

The highest-leverage PM question in any conversation is the one that targets the weakest assumption. Not all of them. Just one.

## Strategic Diagnosis: Finding the Real Problem

Most failed strategies misdiagnose the problem. They attack the visible symptom instead of the structural constraint. They mistake correlation for causation. They inherit last quarter's thesis without asking whether it's still true. The cost of a wrong diagnosis is not that you fail to solve it — it's that you solve something else entirely and wonder why nothing changed.

The first set of eigenquestions exist to surface diagnostic failures:

**"What's the central challenge between us and success — stated specifically enough to be falsifiable?"**

Most strategies fail this test immediately. They name a direction ("be customer-focused," "improve retention," "build faster"), not a diagnosis. A diagnosis is specific enough that you could prove it wrong. If the central challenge is "we lose users because we don't communicate value in the first five minutes," that's falsifiable. You can test it. You can measure it. You can be wrong. If the central challenge is "we need to be better," you cannot.

Listen for vague diagnoses in strategy conversations. They sound like problems but they're not — they're symptoms masquerading as roots. When you hear one, ask the question directly. Force specificity. The conversation will either sharpen or it will expose that nobody knows what the real problem is.

**"What do we believe that most people in our market don't — and what does that belief imply we should do?"**

This separates insight-based strategy from consensus-following. If your strategy is the same as everyone else's, it's not strategy. It's trend-chasing. A real diagnosis often rests on a contrarian view: about your market, your users, your capabilities, or the future. This question surfaces whether that contrarian view exists and whether it actually drives your choices.

If the answer is "we believe everyone underestimates X, so we're going to Y," you have a premise and implications. If the answer is "we're doing what everyone else is doing but with more focus," you don't have strategy — you have a motto.

**"Why can't a well-funded competitor replicate this in 18 months?"**

This question targets the durability of your advantage. Temporary edges are not strategy. They're positioning. If a well-funded, well-run competitor can match you in 18 months, you don't have a moat — you have a head start. And head starts end.

A durable strategy rests on something that's hard to copy: network effects, switching costs, data advantages, organizational capabilities, brand trust, or structural market position. Ask what makes your approach persistent, not just fast. If the answer is "we move faster," ask again. That's not durable.

A strategy without a clear diagnosis is not strategy. It's a direction.

## Guiding Policy: Where Strategy Becomes Real

Diagnosis means nothing without policy. Policy is where strategy becomes real. It's what you say no to. It's the constraint that flows from what you've learned. Most organizations skip it. They jump from "here's what we believe" to "here's what we're building" and lose the force of the diagnosis in the translation.

**"What are we optimizing for — and what are we willing to sacrifice to get it?"**

Listen carefully here. Many teams say they're optimizing for retention while also pushing activation. They say they're optimizing for quality while also shipping fast. They're not being hypocritical — they're failing to make a real choice.

Real optimization is surgical. It's not "we want all of this." It's "under constraint, we chose this." If retention is truly the constraint, what are you willing to sacrifice? Speed? New features? Customer acquisition efficiency? If you won't sacrifice anything, you're not optimizing. You're just hoping.

This question surfaces the real tradeoffs. In the conversation, it often creates discomfort — because making a real choice means someone loses. That discomfort is the signal that the choice is real.

**"What does this strategy rule out?"**

If a strategy doesn't rule anything out, it's not a strategy. It's a mood board. A strategy constrains. It says: because of our diagnosis, because of these tradeoffs, these are things we will not do. Not now, not later, not as side projects.

When you ask this, listen for the quality of the answer. If they say "it rules out distraction," that's vague. If they say "it rules out building in market X, even if the opportunity seems large, because our data infrastructure doesn't support it and building it would require us to sacrifice coherence in market Y," that's a real constraint flowing from a real choice.

**"If we do this, what are we not doing — and what's the cost of that?"**

This surfaces opportunity cost visibility. It's not just about what's ruled out — it's about what else could have been done with the same resources and what value that would have created. Strategy is always choice under constraint. The question is whether you're making the choice consciously.

A team that can articulate the opportunity cost of their strategy — "if we don't build this, here's the revenue or user growth we're trading away" — is thinking strategically. A team that hasn't named the cost is just following a priority list.

A strategy without constraint is not strategy. It's wishful thinking.

## Coherent Action: Where Most Strategies Fail

Here's where most organizational strategy breaks. The diagnosis is sound. The policy is clear. But the initiatives don't reinforce each other. They sit side by side, independent, pursuing the same goal through different logics. That's not coherence. That's sprawl.

Rumelt's observation is blunt: most organizations fail at coherence. The pieces exist. They just don't reinforce each other.

**"Why these initiatives together, why now, why this team?"**

This question surfaces whether items are coordinated or just listed. If you can explain why these specific initiatives together create a coherent whole — because this one lays the foundation, that one builds on it, and this other one captures the value — you have coherence. If each initiative is defensible on its own but their relationship is accidental, you don't.

Listen for the logic of sequencing and reinforcement. Not: "we need better activation, retention, and monetization." But: "we need to solve activation first because it's the constraint on everything else. Once we do that, these users are valuable for retention. Once we've locked in retention, these economics unlock monetization."

**"Which of these items, if it works, changes the trajectory — and which just maintains it?"**

This separates leverage from maintenance. Most roadmaps are 70% maintenance (keep the lights on, run the engine) and 30% leverage (change the trajectory). The question is whether you're clear about which is which and whether you're allocating resources accordingly.

A high-leverage initiative is one where success fundamentally changes what's possible. A maintenance initiative keeps existing things working. Both are necessary. But you cannot spend maintenance energy on leverage problems and expect to change trajectory.

When you ask this question, bad roadmaps reveal themselves. You'll find initiatives that are presented as high-leverage but are actually maintenance with a better narrative.

**"Under what conditions would we stop or pivot this initiative?"**

This surfaces sunk-cost thinking. Real strategy includes an exit condition. If you cannot articulate the conditions under which you would stop investing, you're not thinking strategically. You're committed irrationally.

Good exits are specific: "if after six months we're not seeing improvement in activation, we pivot to a different approach." Bad exits are vague: "we keep going until it works." Vagueness is how initiatives become permanent despite failing.

Coherent action is where most PM strategies fail. The pieces exist. They just don't reinforce each other. The question is whether you're orchestrating them or just running them in parallel.

## How to Use This

Before any strategy review, roadmap planning session, or prioritization discussion: identify the domain — are you diagnosing the right problem, testing whether the policy is real, or checking whether actions cohere? Then find the one eigenquestion that targets the weakest assumption in the room.

Ask it once. Not as an interrogation. Ask it as someone testing whether the group has done its thinking. The answer will either sharpen the strategy or expose where the thinking is incomplete.

The goal is not to ask all of them. It's to find the load-bearing assumption and put weight on it.

Here's a real example: a roadmap planning session where the team had locked in six initiatives for the quarter. The conversation was efficient. Each initiative had a business case. Each had an owner. But when someone asked "if we do this, what are we not doing — and what's the cost of that?" the room went quiet. Because the real constraint wasn't engineering bandwidth or user demand. It was the data infrastructure team. Three of the six initiatives depended on that team. The other three couldn't start until they were done. The roadmap was theoretically sound. But its action was incoherent. The question surfaced what the team had not seen.

The quality of strategy is the quality of the questions being asked. Ask better questions. Get better strategy.
