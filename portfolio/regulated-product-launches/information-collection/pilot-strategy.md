# Piloting in Regulated Environments

Pilots in regulated product launches serve two purposes that most teams conflate: testing the product experience and limiting the firm's regulatory exposure during initial rollout. These are different goals with different success criteria.

When a team conflates them, it usually designs the pilot to test as much as possible with as many customers as possible — which is correct product logic and incorrect regulatory logic. A broader pilot generates more signal but also generates more regulatory surface area. The design has to hold both criteria explicitly, or it satisfies neither well.

---

## How to Scope a Pilot for Regulatory Purposes

"Limited scope" in a regulated context means limiting the customer population and the data set, not the product functionality. A pilot that tests everything with 10 customers gives you less product signal than a pilot that tests one segment thoroughly — but a pilot that expands customer count without restricting segment is not "limited" in any sense legal will recognize.

The scoping conversation has two parts. First, what is the maximum regulatory exposure the firm is willing to accept in the pilot phase? That defines the ceiling on customer count, segment complexity, and transaction volume. Second, within that ceiling, how do you maximize the product signal you can extract? That's where the PM's judgment lives — not in negotiating the ceiling, but in using the space below it effectively.

A pilot scoped for regulatory purposes should have three documented elements before it starts: the specific customer segment included and excluded, the specific features active in the pilot versus held for broader rollout, and the data handling and audit trail mechanism that will be used to demonstrate compliance during the pilot period.

---

## Success Criteria That Satisfy Legal vs. Product

Write both sets of criteria before launch, in the same document, with the same visibility. Don't let them live in separate places where they won't be compared until they conflict.

Legal wants no regulatory incidents during the pilot period and a clean audit trail demonstrating that the product operated within the defined scope. Those are binary criteria. Either the pilot generated a regulatory incident or it didn't. Either the audit trail is complete or it isn't.

Product wants signal on UX, completion rate, and edge cases that didn't surface in testing. Those are directional criteria that require a volume of data to be meaningful. Completion rate from 10 customers is a data point; from 200 customers in a defined segment, it's a signal.

The tension is real: legal's criteria favor small, controlled, low-variance pilots. Product's criteria favor pilots with enough volume to generate statistically meaningful signal. There's no clean resolution — the PM has to make the tradeoff explicit and get agreement from both sides on what the pilot is actually being designed to do.

---

## Segment Sequencing

The first pilot segment should be: the lowest risk profile in the customer population, the best-understood customer type from a regulatory perspective, and the segment the PM has the highest degree of operational control over. Not the biggest segment. Not the highest-value one.

This is counterintuitive to product logic, which typically wants to start with the segment that generates the most signal about product-market fit. But in a regulated pilot, the first segment is a proof point — to legal, to compliance, and to the business — that the product operates cleanly under controlled conditions. That proof point is what enables broader rollout. Optimizing the first segment for signal at the cost of regulatory cleanliness slows the path to the second and third segments.

---

## Three Things That Kill Regulated Pilots

**Scope creep after sign-off.** The most common version: the pilot is running, it's going well, someone says "we're already doing this, let's add one more feature." That feature wasn't in the scope that legal reviewed. Adding it without re-review creates an undocumented deviation from the signed-off design. It feels small in the moment and creates significant exposure later.

**Undefined exit criteria.** Without pre-agreed success criteria, every stakeholder evaluates the pilot through a different lens. The PM who doesn't define exit criteria before launch will negotiate them under pressure when results are ambiguous — and in a regulated pilot, ambiguous results are the norm, not the exception. The stakeholder with the most conservative read of the results will anchor the conversation.

**Over-documentation that creates an audit burden without creating any useful insight.** Some teams, trying to demonstrate rigor, document everything generated by the pilot. This creates a file that no one reads and that, if it contains inconsistencies, creates a liability. Document what was agreed to be documented. Make it accurate. Make it useful. Don't document reflexively.

---

A pilot in a regulated environment is a negotiated instrument. You are not just testing; you are generating evidence that the broader rollout is safe. That changes how you design it.
